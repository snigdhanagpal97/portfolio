## The ask

As dark store assortments grew, a long tail of low performers started taking up limited shelf space, adding replenishment and picking work, and in some cases driving disposal losses. The business needed a scalable way to decide which SKUs stay, which need help, and which can go.

  items={[

    { title: "Long tail expands", body: "Low-velocity SKUs pile up" },
    { title: "Waste increases", body: "Disposal and inventory costs" },
  ]}
/>

<Callout tone="question" label="How might we">
  Reduce assortment inefficiency in dark stores without accidentally delisting SKUs that are new, under-discovered, or strategically important?
</Callout>

## Low sales is not the same as weak

Removing low sellers looks easy from the outside, but it's easy to remove the wrong SKU. A SKU can sell little for reasons that have nothing to do with demand:

<Cards
  items={[
    { title: "New launch", body: "Not enough data to judge yet." },
    { title: "Low availability", body: "Supply held demand back." },
    { title: "Low discoverability", body: "Customers couldn't find it." },
    { title: "Poor pricing", body: "Priced above competitors." },
    { title: "Niche subtype", body: "Needed to keep the category complete." },
    { title: "Repeat-led demand", body: "Small but loyal audience." },
  ]}
/>

The real question was whether low performance was **structural or fixable**. Short shelf life adds a third case: SKUs whose disposal risk outweighs what they sell.

## Principles

I designed it as an explainable funnel rather than one composite score, which made it easier to reason about, validate, and trust.

  vertical
  items={[

    { title: "Distinguish weak from suppressed demand", body: "Before concluding demand is low, check whether availability, discoverability or pricing is holding it back." },

## The signals

The key design decision was keeping **suppression signals** separate from **demand signals**. A SKU with low sales and poor availability isn't weak, it's constrained. For example, availability below 60% counted as suppression, not performance.

<Cards
  items={[
    { tag: "Demand", title: "How much it sells", body: "GMV contribution, units contribution, dark store penetration, subtype penetration." },
    { tag: "Suppression", title: "What holds it back", body: "New SKU check, availability (% days in stock), discoverability (impressions, conversion, visibility)." },
    { tag: "Commercial", title: "Price and loyalty", body: "Price competitiveness vs the lowest competitor price, and repeat purchase index (repeat buyers / unique buyers, 90 days)." },
  ]}
/>

<Callout label="Operational risk">Disposal risk (disposal loss vs absolute margin) and forecast instability (demand variability and shelf life).</Callout>

## The 9-step funnel

Every SKU enters at the top and is checked in order. At each step it either exits with a clear recommendation or moves on. Only SKUs that fail all nine checks are recommended for delisting.

## Disposal override

Perishable and short shelf-life SKUs needed separate treatment. Some weren't just weak, they actively lost money because expected sell-through didn't justify the disposal risk. A SKU can convert decently and still lose money if its shelf life doesn't match how fast it sells.

## How it ran

The framework ran monthly on the 28th. Each category manager got an email with their category's watchlist and delist recommendations, and the reasoning behind each flag.

## What I'd build next

V1 was kept explainable and rule-based on purpose, so category managers could trust it and override it. Later versions move from flagging weak SKUs to optimising what replaces them.
